Agentic Discovery of Soundness Bugs in Safe Rust Bindings

📅 25 Nov 2026 | ⏱️ 2 min read (312 words, 📖 Standard) | Source: Arxiv

Industry observers note that arXiv:2611.31813v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Rust crates confine unsafe code, such as calls into C or C++ libraries or system calls, behind safe interfaces that must uphold Rust's invariants. If an interface encodes one rule of the code beneath it too weakly, a program written in purely safe Rust can cause undefined behavior. Static analyzers and fuzzers are bound to fixed patterns or to the paths they execute. We propose LLM agents as auditors of these interfaces, and study how well they do it. We first define a taxonomy of the defects of such interfaces, including undefined behavior, divergences from the wrapped library, and incomplete unsafe contracts. We then measure, in repeated audits of a real-world crate by a closed and an open model, Claude Opus 5.5 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, which defect classes agents find, how well they triage them, and what makes them find more. Agents find the first two classes unprompted, but find few incomplete contracts even when asked. Their findings are almost always real, even without an independent reviewer, yet they lack triage discipline: they seldom confirm with a sanitizer, and they stop early. With the same guidance, the open model finds as many defects as the closed one at a quarter of the cost. We integrate these insights in Crustify, an agentic system for automating C-to-Rust tasks, and run it at scale. In 16 widely used crates, including the bindings to the C libraries OpenSSL, SQLite, libgit2, and libcurl and to the C++ engine V8, Crustify found 225 undefined-behavior bugs, 23 behavioral divergences, and 36 incomplete unsafe contracts, between 3 and 61 per crate at a median cost under $50 per audit. In the Linux kernel's Rust abstractions, it found 11 more undefined-behavior bugs and 4 incomplete contracts. Maintainers have fixed 26 of the 83 findings that we reported.

How Far Apart Are Two Proofs? Measuring Proof Dissimilarity in a Proof Kernel and in Lean/Mathlib

📅 25 Nov 2026 | ⏱️ 1 min read (287 words, 📖 Standard) | Source: Arxiv

⏱️ ~1 mins left to finish
📖 0% read

arXiv:2611.31811v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: We now live in the age of machine-generated mathematics: AI systems produce research-level proofs faster than the human mathematical community can read them. Verification is only part of the challenge. We also need to know whether a "new proof" is indeed new, whether a thousand generated proofs contain one idea or many, and where each sits relative to human mathematics. All of these require measuring distances between proofs. Following Hilbert's 24th problem and the identity-of-proofs problem, we ask how far apart two proofs of one theorem are, and what shape the proof space they inhabit has. We define 19 dissimilarity measures in 6 families (syntactic, structural, dependency & resource, semantic, information-theoretic, higher-categorical) and 10 desirable properties, which we argue are the conditions for safely optimising a measure. In a Curry-Howard kernel, 5 machine-checked proofs that the primes are infinite show that raw syntax cannot tell a rewrite from a new argument, that dependency-based and structural measures reproduce the intuitive ordering (Euclid rewritten < Goldbach < Furstenberg), and that semantic measures invert it: Furstenberg's topological proof produces exactly Euclid's witness. A replication in Lean 4/Mathlib (3 theorems, 19 proofs, 18 measures) confirms that dependency-closure measures track intuition best, and shows that equality of normal forms never holds between differently written versions of the same argument, that proofs by citation look unlike the arguments they cite, and that proof irrelevance cannot distinguish any two proofs. We argue for reporting dissimilarity as a multi-level profile, extend the measures to proofs of different theorems, and discuss uses, from detecting duplicated AI proofs and orchestrating prover agents to interpreting AI proofs via their nearest human-understandable counterparts. Code, Lean sources and data are released.

📅 25 Nov 2026 | ⏱️ 1 min read (197 words, 📖 Standard) | Source: Arxiv

⏱️ ~1 mins left to finish

arXiv:2611.31801v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Brainprint authentication enables continuous identity verification, but EEG data are privacy-sensitive. Existing feature-level encrypted authentication (FLEA) schemes protect extracted features under a trusted-client assumption. We show theoretically and empirically that a compromised client with access to plaintext query features and continuous authentication scores can exactly recover a registered template after finitely many interactions. To address this risk, we propose TopoCryptoEEG, a homomorphic-encryption-based framework that encrypts preprocessed EEG data at the client and performs feature extraction, template matching, score computation, and threshold centering on ciphertexts at the server. This extends protection from extracted features to preprocessed EEG data and removes the client-side plaintext-feature interface exploited by the attack. For efficient encrypted authentication, we develop Multi-Window Topology-Aware Encoding (MWTE) and a Topographic Branch Model with Linear Fusion (TopoFuse-MLP) to preserve EEG spatial information while limiting multiplicative depth. Query-Patch Coordinate Packing (QPCP) and Lane-Preserving BSGS Evaluation (LP-BSGS) further reduce ciphertext operations. Experiments on HGD and BCICIV-2a show high authentication accuracy, with homomorphic computation taking 1-2 s on CPUs and about 14 ms on GPUs. TopoCryptoEEG achieves over 18x average speedup over the EER (IMWUT 2025) baseline, supporting practical privacy-preserving brainprint authentication.

It has been revealed that arXiv:2611.31767v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Architects have two mature instruments for performance work. Top-Down analysis tells them where a design loses its pipeline slots, and design-space exploration (DSE) tells them which configuration scores best. Neither explains why a search stops improving: why a backend-bound design rejects a prefetcher that the bottleneck label recommends, or why a capacity increase that helped in one configuration hurts in the next. Unlike evolutionary LLM search, which keeps only high-scoring programs, DELTA keeps failed edges as evidence. This article gives you the behind-the-scenes of how we made an efficient inference server that powers bloom. inference server that powers https://huggingface.co/bigscience/bloom.

Recent findings suggest that we achieved a 5x latency reduction over several weeks (and 50x more throughput). We wanted to share all the struggles and epic wins we went through to achieve such speed improvements.

It has been revealed that A lot of different people were involved at many stages so not everything will be covered here. And please bear with us, some of the content might be outdated or flat out wrong because we're still learning how to optimize extremely large models and lots of new hardware features and content keep coming out regularly.

If your favorite flavor of optimizations is not discussed or improperly represented, we're sorry, please share it with us we're more than happy to try out newly introduced stuff and correct our mistakes.

This goes without saying but without the large model being accessible in the first place, there would be no real reasons to optimize inference for it. This was an incredible effort led by many different people.

Industry observers note that to maximize the GPU during training, several solutions were explored and in the end, Megatron-Deepspeed was chosen to train the end model. This meant that the code as-is wasn't necessarily compatible with the transformers library.

Because of the original training code, we set out to do something which we regularly do: port an existing model to transformers. The goal was to extract from the training code the relevant parts and implement it within transformers. This effort was tackled by Younes. This is by no means a small effort as it took almost a month and 200 commits to get there.

There are several things to note that will come back later:.

We needed to have smaller models bigscience/bigscience-small-testing and bigscience/bloom-560m. This is extremely important because they are smaller, so everything is faster when working with them.

First, you have to abandon all hope to have exactly the same logits at the end down to the bytes. PyTorch versions can change the kernels and introduce subtle differences, and different hardware might yield different results because of different architecture (and you probably don't want to develop on a A100 GPU all the time for cost reasons).

Getting a good strict test suite is really important for all models.

The best test we found was having a fixed set of prompts. You know the prompt, you know the completion that needs to be deterministic so greedy. If two generations are identical, you can basically ignore small logits differences Whenever you see a drift, you need to investigate. It could be that your code is not doing what it should OR that you are actually out of domain for that model and therefore the model is more sensitive to noise. If you have several prompts and long enough prompts, you're less likely to trigger that for all prompts by accident. The more prompts the better, the longer the better.

Recent findings suggest that the first model (small-testing) is in bfloat16 like the big bloom so everything should be very similar, but it wasn't trained a lot or just doesn't perform well, so it highly fluctuates in outputs. That means we had issues with those generation tests. The second model is more stable but was trained and saved in float16 instead of bfloat16. That's more room for error between the two.

To be perfectly fair bfloat16 -> float16 conversion seemed to be OK in inference mode (bfloat16 mostly exists to handle large gradients, which do not exist in inference).

It has been revealed that during that step, one important tradeoff was discovered and implemented. Because bloom was trained in a distributed setting, part of the code was doing Tensor parallelism on a Linear layer meaning running the same operation as a single operation on a single GPU was giving different results. This took a while to pinpoint and either we went for 100% compliance and the model was much slower, or we would take a small difference in generation but was much faster to run and simpler code. We opted for a configurable flag.

Now we have a workable transformers clean version of the start working on running this.

Bloom is a 352GB (176B parameters in bf16) model, we need at least that much GPU RAM to make it fit. We briefly explored offloading to CPU on smaller machines but the inference speed was orders of magnitude slower so we discarded it.

According to recent reports, then we wanted to basically use the pipeline. So it's dogfooding and this is what the API uses under the hood all the time.

Sources indicate that however pipelines are not distributed aware (it's not their goal). After briefly discussing options, we ended up using accelerate newly created device_map="auto" to manage the sharding of the model. We had to iron out a few bugs, and fix the transformers code a bit to help accelerate do the right job.

It works by splitting the various layers of the transformers and giving part of the model to each GPU. So GPU0 gets to work, then hands it over to GPU1 so on and so forth.

In the end, with a small HTTP server on top, we could start serving bloom (the significant model) !!

But we haven't even started discussing optimizations yet!

We actually have quite a bit, all this process is a castle of cards. During optimizations we are going to make modifications to the underlying code, being extra sure you're not killing the model in one way or the other is really notable and easier to do than you think.

Recent findings suggest that so we are now at the very first step of optimizations and we need to start measuring and keep measuring performance. So we need to consider what we care about. For an open inference server supporting many options, we expect customers to send many queries with different parameters and what we care about are:.

The number of consumers we can serve at the same time (throughput) How long does it take for an average user to be served (latency)?

We made a testing script in locust which is exactly this:.

**Note: This is not the best nor the only load testing we used, but it was always the first to be run so that it could compare fairly across approaches. Being the best on this benchmark does NOT mean it is the best solution. Other more complex scenarios had to be used in addition to actual real-world performance. **.

Industry observers note that we wanted to observe the ramp-up for various implementations and also make sure that underload the server properly circuit breaked. Circuit breaking means that the server can answer (fast) that it will not answer your query because too many people are trying to use it at the same time. It's extremely important to avoid the hug of death.

On this benchmark the initial performance was (on 16xA100 40Go on GCP which is the machine used throughout):.

Requests/s : 0.3 (throughput) Latency: 350ms/token (latency).

Those numbers are not that great. Before getting to work let's estimate the best we can imagine achieving. The formula for amount of operations is 24Bsh^2 + 4𝐵s^2h24Bsh^2 + 4𝐵s^2h where B is the batch size, s the sequence length, and h the hidden dimension.

Let's do the math and we are getting 17 TFlop for a single forward pass. Looking at the specs of A100 it claims 312 TFLOPS for a single card. That means a single GPU could potentially run at 17 / 312 = 54ms/token. We're using 16 of those so 3ms/token on the overall machine. Take all these numbers with a big grain of salt, it's never possible to reach those numbers, and real-life performance rarely matches the specs. Also if computation is not your limiting factor then this is not the lowest you can get. It's just good practice to know how far you are from your target. In this case, we're 2 orders of magnitude so pretty far. Also, this estimate puts all the flops at the service of latency which means only a single request can go at a time (it's ok since you're maximizing your machine so there's not much else to be done, but we can have higher latency and get throughput back through batching much more easily).

Now that we have a good understanding of where we stand it's time to get to work.

We tried many different things based on the people and our various knowledge.

Sources indicate that ALL endeavors deserve their own blog post so I'll just list them, explain the few final learnings and delve into the details of only what went into the current server. Moving from Pipeline Parallelism (PP) to Tensor Parallelism (TP) is one substantial interesting change for latency. Each GPU will own part of the parameters and all will be working at the same time. So the latency should decrease drastically but the price to pay is the communication overhead since they regularly need to communicate with each other about their results.

It is to note that this is a very wide range of approaches and the intent was deliberately to learn more about each tool and how it could fit in later endeavors.

In a recent development, ok, we have removed most of the low-hanging fruits now we went roughly from 350ms/token latency to 300ms/token in PP. The latency went directly from 300ms/token to 91ms/token which is a huge improvement in user experience. A simple 20 tokens request went from 6s to 2s which went from a "slow" experience to slightly delayed.

Also, the throughput went up a lot to 10RPS. The throughput comes from the fact that running a query in batch_size=1 takes the same time as batch_size=32 and throughput becomes essentially free in latency cost at this point.

Recent findings suggest that now that we had a TP implementation, we could start profiling and optimizing again. It's a significant enough shift that we had to start from scratch again.

According to recent reports, the first thing that stood out, is that synchronization (ncclAllReduce) starts to become a preponderant part of the load, which is expected, this is the synchronization part and it is taking some time. We never tried to look and optimize this as it's already using nccl but there might still be some room for improvement there. We assumed it would be hard to do much better.

The second thing is that Gelu operator was launching many elementwise kernels and overall it was taking a bigger share of compute than we expected.

This transforms the operations from multiple small element-wise kernels (and hence tensor copies) to a single kernel operation!

This provided a 10% latency improvement from 91ms/token to 81ms/token, right there!

Be careful though, this is not some magic black box you can just throw everywhere, the kernel fusion will not necessarily happen or the previously used operations are already extremely efficient.

That was the epic fail because removing it gave us back the 25% and then both codes ran just as fast. Sources indicate that another place where we had to be extra careful, was the initial forward pass (without past) and the later forward passes (with past). Industry observers note that just like the Rust counterpart, we had to implement the batching of requests with different parameters. Since we were in the PyTorch world, we have pretty much full control of what's going on. Since we're in Python, we have the limiting factor that the torch.distributed needs to run on several processes instead of threads, which means it's slightly harder to communicate between processes. So we reconstructed a generate function that takes a list of parameters and applies them to each member of the batch.

Another really important aspect of the final UX is latency. Since we have different parameter sets for different requests, we might have.

If we were batching all the way, we would be making the user that asked to wait for 18s and making it appear to him as if we were running at 900ms/token which is quite slow!

We also happen to save 230 tokens worth of computation.

So flexibility is notable to get the best possible latency out there.
