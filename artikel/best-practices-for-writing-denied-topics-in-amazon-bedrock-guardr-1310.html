According to recent reports, note: Denied Topic structure in Standard tier. A topic has three parts, a name, a definition, and up to five examples, and the guardrail evaluates all three together as a single unit, not the definition alone. The name can be up to 100 characters. The definition can be up to 1,000 characters, with 100–300 as the practical sweet spot. Each example can be up to 100 characters, and a guardrail can hold up to 30 topics.

Recent findings suggest that this practice sets the general frame. Best practices 2 through 4 apply it to each part of a topic in turn: the name, the definition, and the examples.

Industry observers note that A frequent reason a topic blocks legitimate queries is that the topic describes a subject domain rather than what the user wants from the exchange. Two queries in the same domain can carry different intentions. “When does my direct deposit clear?” and “How much does an L5 engineer earn?” both concern pay, but only the second is a salary comparison. A domain-framed topic collapses those distinctions and can block queries semantically near the domain. A topic framed around the user’s intention matches only queries where that intention is present.

Sources indicate that intention is the framing lens. To make it operational, name the topic after the specific action or content type that expresses the intention on the surface, such as a request, a claim, or a piece of generated content. Intention tells you what to catch, and action gives you a concrete shape the guardrail can evaluate.

It has been revealed that the guardrail evaluates both the topic name and its definition together. When either component names a domain, the topic matches too broadly. When both name an action that expresses a specific intention, the topic is precise.

Recent findings suggest that the next few best practices apply this idea to AnyCompany Bank’s own topics, with concrete examples. Best practice 2 renames them, and Best practice 3 tightens their definitions to match.

The topic name has a meaningful effect on what is matched, often greater than expected. A broad category name (Finance, Security, Legal) can lead the guardrail to associate the topic with vocabulary in that category, even when the definition attempts to narrow the scope. A specific action name (Investment Recommendations, Hacking Instructions, Suing for Damages) helps narrow the scope before the definition is read.

Examples teach the guardrail where the boundaries of your topic are. Not every example does this equally well. Five paraphrases of the same idea teach the guardrail nothing fresh, while five carefully chosen examples can draw the outline of the topic with much less definition text. The examples that carry the most weight are the ones sitting near the topic’s edge, close enough to a plausible non-match that they show the guardrail where to draw the line. We call these boundary examples. A good boundary example has three properties. First, it’s inside the topic’s scope. Second, it’s close to a plausible non-match, using vocabulary or structure that resembles inputs the topic should not match. Third, it differs from your other examples in sub-case, sentence shape, and vocabulary.

Some concepts are inherently ambiguous, because the same words describe both a matching and non-matching action, depending on context. In these cases, add an explicit anchor that fixes the context: an institutional setting, a stated purpose, or a dual condition of the form “only applies when both X and Y are present”.

In a recent development, A guardrail evaluates its topics together, not one at a time. When two topics have similar meanings, they interfere with each other. It becomes difficult to predict which topic the guardrail reports as the match, and the combined effect can block legitimate inputs that neither topic alone would block. Overlap between topics is often the reason a well-written topic produces incorrect results. The problem isn’t that topic, but a neighboring topic.

Denied Topics is the right tool when the rule you need to enforce is specific to your business and expressible as a short description of the content. Think of a regulatory line, a policy commitment, a legal risk, or a scope boundary, rather than a universal safety category or a fixed list of words. Guardrails offers several policies that share the same goal of blocking specific content, but each is designed for a different kind of signal. Using Denied Topics for a category that another policy handles natively tends to block too much or too little, complicates your configuration, and produces worse results. A carefully written topic for a category that Content Filter already covers is usually less effective than Content Filter for that category.

Note that enabling more than one policy in the same guardrail incurs additional cost, because each enabled policy is evaluated on every request. When you compose Denied Topics with Content Filter or Prompt Attack, plan for the combined cost.

A short trailing exclusion isn’t a general-purpose fix for every false refusal. It reliably rules out queries that are categorically different from the topic, such as a different intent, a different asker, or a different type of content. It’s less reliable when a benign query is worded almost exactly like the definition’s positive language, only about a different object. In that case, the exclusion competes against wording it closely resembles, and the match often wins.

In a recent development, even a guardrail that follows every practice in this post won’t produce fully deterministic results across every possible input. Denied Topics reads meaning with a language model, and language-model evaluations carry some inherent variance. There are two forms of this variance you should plan for.

Recent findings suggest that variance when repeating the exact same request. Sending the identical input to the identical guardrail configuration two times in a row can, in rare cases, produce different results. The same characters go in, the same topic list applies, everything is the same, and yet one call blocks and the next passes. For most inputs, and for topics that follow the preceding guidance, this doesn’t happen, and the result is stable across repeated calls. The exceptions are borderline inputs, whose meaning sits close to the semantic edge of a topic. Those are the ones prone to flipping on repeat. The more precisely a topic is defined (action-scoped, specific objects, clear anchor, boundary examples), the fewer of your inputs fall in that borderline zone.

Variance when the topic list changes. The guardrail evaluates all topics together with the input on every request. Changes to the topic list, such as adding a topic, removing one, or reordering the same topics, can shift results for previously-tested inputs. An input that was blocked with one topic list might pass with a different one. This effect happens more when two topics share meaning or when a topic definition is broad. It happens less when every topic in the list has a specific, action-scoped definition and clear boundary examples. Applying the practices in this post meaningfully reduces both forms of variance.

Denied Topics is a powerful policy. With it, you can describe a bespoke content category in a short natural-language definition, without training a classifier or hand-crafting regular expressions. That power depends on describing the category the way the guardrail reads it: as the user’s intention, expressed as a specific action on a specific object, not as a subject domain. Get the framing right and the rest of the practices in this post, such as naming, definition, examples, anchors, overlap management, and policy selection, fall into place naturally.
